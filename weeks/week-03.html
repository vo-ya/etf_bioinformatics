15–40 / SamplingMean depth and covered fraction

Depth at a position is the number of reads covering it. Mean depth averages these counts over the target. Breadth of coverage is the fraction of target positions covered at least once.

For N full reads of length L from a target of length G, mean depth is N × L / G. With unequal lengths, use the sum of read lengths divided by G. This arithmetic assumes the counted bases belong to the target. In real data, target length, contamination, and ambiguous placements can complicate the estimate.

Worked comparison

Both datasets contain 12 read bases, so the mean is 12 / 12 = 1×. Spread reads give depth 1 everywhere and 100% breadth. Clustered reads give depth 3 at the first four positions and depth 0 at eight positions: 33.3% breadth. Repeating the same sampled region does not supply its missing neighbours.

The toy simulator knows the read positions because it generated them. An assembler does not receive those true positions. Random starts are sampled within a finite linear target; its end positions have fewer possible covering starts. The simulator omits the biases of library preparation and sequencing.

Library preparation and sequence composition can make some regions harder to observe. Repeatedly sampling the accessible regions increases mean depth while leaving gaps elsewhere.

Calculate depth before taking its mean

Three four-base reads start at positions 0, 2, and 6 on a twelve-base teaching target. Their depth profile is 1,1,2,2,1,1,1,1,1,1,0,0. The sum is twelve observed bases, so target-wide mean depth is 12/12 = 1×. Ten positions have nonzero depth, so breadth is 10/12 ≈ 83.3%. Averaging only over covered positions would instead give 12/10 = 1.2×; that changes the denominator.

Complete breadth does not establish fragment order

The simulator knows that ATGA, CCTG, and GTAC start at 0, 4, and 8 in ATGACCTGGTAC. Every base is observed once. An assembler receiving just the read strings does not receive those positions. No pair shares a suffix–prefix overlap of at least three bases; both ATGA|CCTG|GTAC and ATGA|GTAC|CCTG contain all three reads and have total length twelve. A read crossing a boundary, such as GACC across the first displayed join, supplies linking information absent from the three separate fragments.

Contigs and candidate joins

A contig is an assembled contiguous sequence without an unresolved gap represented inside it. It may be shorter than a chromosome. Being contiguous does not guarantee correctness: an unsupported join can make a longer, incorrect contig.

Discuss the join / 10 minutes

Two groups choose different tied joins. Each can show a perfect overlap. What additional observation would help decide between them? Consider a longer read covering unique sequence on both sides, independent supporting molecules, or stopping at the ambiguity. Explain why merely choosing the first read in a file is insufficient.

Real assemblers must also handle contained reads, reverse-complement orientations, and disagreements between overlapping observations.

Test a candidate against every input read

The reads ATGA, TGAC, and TGAT all occur in both ATGATGAC (eight bases) and ATGACATGAT (ten bases). Locate each read in both strings. Containing every exact read is a useful consistency test, but it does not establish a unique answer. A rule preferring shorter reconstructions selects the first of these candidates; the rule is a computational objective, not proof that the true sequence has minimum length. Repeated sequence and unsampled intervals can make a true target longer. An observation linking the disputed contexts would test the proposed order.

85–125 / A graph of short wordsAn edge-labelled de Bruijn graph

A k-mer is a consecutive word of k bases. Sliding a three-base window through ATGACCT gives ATG, TGA, GAC, ACC, and CCT. There are 7 − 3 + 1 = 5 windows. Count each read separately: never invent a word spanning the boundary between two unrelated reads.

We use an edge-labelled de Bruijn graph. A node is a distinct (k−1)-base word. A k-mer becomes a directed edge from its first k−1 bases to its last k−1 bases. Thus ATG gives AT → TG. This is one common graph convention; some descriptions instead put k-mers on nodes.

The visual workbench requires every entered read to be at least k bases long. In the general counting rule and the homework, a shorter read simply contributes zero windows.

Worked path and support

The simple example has six nodes and five distinct edges. The path AT → TG → GA → AC → CC → CT spells ATGACCT. With the two overlapping reads, GAC is observed twice; the five distinct edges stay the same. Here, the two observations of GAC come from the same location in the teaching target.

In unique-edge mode, the workbench permits each distinct edge once in its restricted traversal exercise. Its optional occurrence mode displays each read-observed window separately; that graph need not admit one complete walk. In an ideal complete spectrum with known occurrence multiplicities, an Eulerian traversal uses every edge occurrence once. Read-derived counts mix repeated sampling with repeated genomic sequence. They therefore need a sampling model before they can be used as genomic occurrence multiplicities.

Choosing k

Smaller k retains less context and can merge more repeated words. Larger k can distinguish more context, but each read contributes fewer windows and a sequencing error disrupts more overlapping windows. If k exceeds a read’s length, that read contributes no k-mers. The choice depends on read length, errors, and the repeated contexts that need to be distinguished.

Predict before changing k / 5 minutes

For each five-base read, how many windows exist at k = 3, 4, and 5? Which words from the two reads still overlap? Explain why making k larger cannot recover sequence that was never sampled.

Background: Zerbino and Birney, Velvet (2008) describes assembly using de Bruijn graphs and graph simplification. The workbench illustrates the graph representation on small inputs; Velvet also performs graph simplification and assembly steps.

Pooling counts does not join read strings

Count windows within ATGAC and within GACCT, then combine the counts. Each read supplies three windows at k=3, giving six observed windows. Concatenating the raw records as ATGAC|GACCT creates eight windows, including the invented boundary-crossing words ACG and CGA. Neither was observed within a read. This concatenation also differs from the overlap-based merge ATGACCT.

Identify what a node and an edge represent

In the read-overlap graph, ATGAC and GACCT are whole-read nodes; their connection records the shared suffix–prefix word GAC. In our de Bruijn convention at k=3, GA and AC are nodes, and GAC is a directed edge with observed support two. A read overlap and a short-word edge encode different objects.

Validate membership as well as compatible letters

From the node TG, both proposed pairs TG→GA and TG→GC share the required boundary letter G. Their implied words are TGA and TGC. Only TGA occurs in our observed inventory ATG,TGA,GAC,ACC,CCT. A path through the observed graph must use its actual edges; letter compatibility alone does not create a missing edge.

Changing k changes which connections are observed

For the same reads ATGAC and GACCT, k=4 yields edges ATGA,TGAC,GACC,ACCT and the connected path ATG→TGA→GAC→ACC→CCT. At k=5 there are only two edges: ATGA→TGAC and GACC→ACCT. Connecting them would require the word TGACC, which occurs in the known teaching target but within neither observed read. More context per edge comes with fewer windows per read; increasing k cannot supply a missing observation.

One changed base affects overlapping windows

Change the middle G in the known teaching sequence ATGAC to T, giving ATTAC. At k=3 the words ATG,TGA,GAC become ATT,TTA,TAC. All three windows include the middle base, so a single read error changes all three. They are correlated consequences of one error. Changing the first base would affect only one window. Without known truth, alternative words could also reflect genuine variation; their origin needs evidence about base quality and the molecules that were sampled.

Decide, inspect evidence, reconsider / 8 minutes

Would you remove the smaller branch?

This invented graph has two paths from AT to AA. At k = 3, each edge joins a two-base prefix to a two-base suffix. The paths spell ATGAA and ATCAA. Twelve read records span the first path; three span the second. These are records spanning a whole path. We have not yet established how many independent input molecules they represent.

  1. Before opening the evidence, choose: remove the smaller branch, retain both for now, or request another measurement. Give a reason.
  2. Read the two alternative follow-ups below. In which case would removing the branch be more defensible? What could still go wrong?
  3. Would your recommendation change if the goal were one representative consensus rather than retaining genuine variation?

The two follow-ups describe alternative hypothetical datasets with the same initial 12:3 counts. They are separate from the cultured-cell case.

Follow-up A: low-confidence copies of one molecule

The discriminating C has low base-call confidence. All three records have the same orientation and fragment endpoints, and a validated molecular tag identifies them as copies of one input molecule. The available longer independent observations support only the upper path.

The three records provide weak evidence about one input molecule’s sequence. Filtering the branch may be reasonable for a consensus task, provided the discarded alternative is documented. One genuine rare molecule could also have been amplified, so the remaining uncertainty concerns that original molecule’s sequence.

Follow-up B: three independently supported molecules

The discriminating C has high base-call confidence. Validated molecular information identifies three distinct input molecules, the supporting fragments include both orientations after strand normalization, and longer independent reads link this alternative to the same flanking context.

The independent observations and shared flanks support a real sequence alternative. It could be an allele or a rare component of a mixture. Distinguishing those explanations requires information about the sample. Retain and report the alternative when the task is to preserve variation.

Choosing a consensus or retaining variation

The 12:3 ratio becomes useful only with evidence about the distinguishing bases and the molecules behind the records. Strand support and longer linking reads help test whether the alternative belongs in the same sequence context. A consensus may choose one representative and document the unresolved alternative. A reconstruction intended to retain variation should preserve a credible minority path.

A scaffold orders and orients contigs using linking evidence; unresolved gaps can remain between them. A scaffold’s span is not the same as a continuously determined sequence. Finishing a report should include what was reconstructed, what is missing, and what evidence supports the joins.

Measure contiguity, question correctness / 10 minutes

Does a higher N50 settle the comparison?

Sort contig lengths from largest to smallest. Add lengths until their cumulative total reaches at least half the assembly’s total length. N50 is the length of the contig that reaches that threshold; L50 is the number of contigs used to reach it.

  1. Calculate N50 for lengths 8, 7, and 5 before opening the calculator.
  2. Compare an assembly with lengths 12 and 8. Both totals are 20. Which N50 is larger?
  3. Suppose the 12-base contig contains an incorrect join. Does the calculation detect it?

Worked values and limits

For 8, 7, 5, total length is 20 and the halfway threshold is 10. The first contig contributes 8; the first two contribute 15. N50 is 7 bases and L50 is 2. For 12, 8, the first contig already crosses 10: N50 is 12 and L50 is 1. Neither result checks whether the letters or joins are correct.

N50 uses the assembly’s own total. Sequence absent from the assembly is absent from that denominator too. Report total length and contig count, and check the sequence evidence separately: even an incorrect join contributes its full length to N50.

Definition reference: NCBI assembly statistics. Compare contig statistics with contig statistics; scaffold statistics may include unresolved spans.

Linking contigs can leave sequence undetermined

A contig is a continuous assembled sequence. A scaffold uses linking evidence to order and orient contigs while allowing unresolved gaps. In the schematic below, linking evidence supports placing ATGACCT before TTAG in the displayed orientations, but the intervening sequence and gap length are unspecified. The drawing’s spacing is not a length measurement. Additional observations spanning the interval would be needed to determine the missing sequence; the two contigs should not simply be concatenated into a gapless contig.

Removing a contig can raise N50

For lengths [8,7,5], total length is 20, the halfway threshold is 10, and N50 is 7. Delete the five-base contig: [8,7] totals 15, so the threshold becomes 7.5. The eight-base contig now crosses that threshold by itself: N50 rises to 8 and L50 falls to 1. No remaining contig grew. If the deleted contig was genuine distinct target sequence, completeness declined even as N50 increased. Report total length and investigate what was removed rather than treating the larger statistic as sufficient evidence of improvement.

Request an observation that spans the proposed join

Joining ATGAC and GACCT through their shared GAC gives ATGACCT. Another observation of GAC adds support to the shared word without linking the flanks. A forward, exact read TGACC contains the left T, shared GAC, and right C in one observation. It supports that local adjacency and supplies the missing k=5 edge from the graph example. Repeated contexts can still make this short word nonunique, so independent molecules and longer distinguishing context may be needed to assess the genomic join.
