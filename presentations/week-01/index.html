DNA sequencing measures base order

We want to determine the order of A, C, G, and T
in DNA molecules from a sample.

Input

Measurement

Output
Software estimates the bases. Each reported sequence is a read, such as ACGTACGT.

Companion lesson11

Use three minutes to introduce the measurement before naming platforms or library components. The biological question is whether DNA sequences differ; the laboratory task is to determine base order. A sample contains many molecules. A fragment is physical DNA; a read is the sequence reported from a measurement. The instrument records a signal, and base-calling software infers letters. A read often covers only part of a genome and may contain errors. Its sequence alone does not establish where it belongs or what a difference does biologically.

Reveal measurement, then output. Ask what could change a called base: noise in the measured signal can make the inferred letter wrong even when the original DNA is unchanged. Preparation can also introduce artifacts. We will trace preparation, compare sensors, and test a noise example. The short string is illustrative, not a measurement from either culture. Source for the definition: NHGRI: DNA sequencing. Companion explanation.

Trace fragment origin ↗12

Use five minutes after the sequencing introduction. Define a library as the prepared collection of molecules an instrument can read. The insert is sample-derived DNA; adapters are added sequences. This diagram is one short-read workflow, not a universal protocol for all platforms. Spend two minutes tracing adapters/insert and read-through, one on paired ends, and two on the unit-counting prompt. Adapters supply instrument/primer sites; a short insert can let sequencing enter the opposite adapter. Adapter sequence can have high quality and is not genomic insert sequence. PCR is optional in preparation. Both mate outputs are 5′→3′ but run in opposite orientations along one fragment. A 500-base insert with two 150-base reads can leave a 200-base unread middle; two records do not guarantee a continuous 300-base sequence.

Ask: one original fragment from one culture gives three PCR copies; suppose each yields a pair. Answer: six read records, one original fragment, one source culture, zero new independent cultures. The mates share a fragment and the pairs share molecular origin. They are technical observations, not biological replicates. The later independent confidence-comparison Read 1/Read 2 examples are not this paired-end illustration.

Try the signal experiment ↗13

Use six minutes to compare the three measurement principles. Connect each row to the introduction: physical DNA, measured signal, reported read. Use the optical signal control to distinguish light intensity from the inferred base, then turn to the nanopore close-up on slide 14. Explain that 2 × 150 bp gives two reads of the kind introduced in the preparation example. NGS means next-generation sequencing; it is a broad term, not a synonym for Illumina. This table compares conventional Illumina short reads with Oxford Nanopore and PacBio HiFi long reads. Illumina uses cycles of sequencing by synthesis and fluorescent detection of clusters; exact channel designs differ. Nanopore current depends on several bases in the sensing region, not one fixed current per base. PacBio single-molecule real-time (SMRT) sequencing detects fluorescent incorporation by a polymerase; circular consensus combines repeated observations of the same insert. Repeated passes are not independent cultures.

Read lengths depend on library design and chemistry. HiFi consensus accuracy differs from the accuracy of an individual pass. Avoid a fixed platform accuracy ranking. If discussing a repeat, define it as a sequence occurring in more than one genomic location. Ask which read could connect the unique flanks around a 5 kb repeat: a sufficiently long read from either long-read platform could; a single 150-base read cannot span it. This is a length argument, not a guarantee of correct assembly. Preserve the separate point that paired-end reads may leave an unread middle.

Primary references: Illumina sequencing by synthesis; Oxford Nanopore sensing; PacBio HiFi and circular consensus. Companion comparison. Optical signal experiment.

Quality estimates the chance of a wrong call

Predict: what happens to estimated error when Q rises by 10?

Q10Q20Q30Q40

1% estimated error per base call
About 1 in 100 calls, if calibrated.

Q = −10 log10(perror)   ⇔   perror = 10−Q/10

perror is the estimated error probability (0–1).
Q20: 1% · Q30: 0.1% · Q40: 0.01%.

Companion lesson23

Move the slider from Q20 to Q30 and Q40 after students predict. Arrow keys change the focused slider without advancing the slide. Discuss frequencies across calls rather than guaranteeing an outcome for a particular base. Q20 estimates probability 0.01 and Q30 estimates 0.001. A ten-point rise reduces estimated error probability tenfold. Define p_error as the estimated probability that a base call is wrong, written as a fraction rather than a percentage. At Q30, p_error = 10^(−30/10) = 0.001 = 0.1%. Use the equation to explain why adding 10 to Q divides the estimated error probability by 10. Character encoding remains optional reference material and is used in homework. Optional original encoding figure. Quality-score reference. Companion lesson.
